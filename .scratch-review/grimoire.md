# The grimoire

*Being the names this world keeps of itself, gathered and set down in one book,
and every name in it checked against the last.*

## Of this book

A grimoire is not a book of spells. It is the book of the names that spells are
made of. A spell is a sentence, and a sentence is names held together, and the
ruin of most magic is not weakness but poverty: a caster with three words trying
to say a thing that wants nine.

So this is the whole of it. Every name any hand could find is set down here
without ornament, in the order a student meets them — the ground underfoot
first, then the doers upon it, then the bonds between them, then time, then
worth, then notions, then the inward eye, then the passions.

It is complete. I have been careful about that. Where two names are near enough
to be confused I have set them beside one another and let the difference do the
work. Where a name is given by its contrary I have said so, because a name given
by its opposite will not carry. Where a name is longer than a breath I have kept
it only if a person might say it and be understood by another person.

The ties are at the end. They are the smaller half of the book and the larger
half of the craft.

## Of names, and what holds them

A name by itself is no working. A name is a **rod**: it pushes, it stands off
from the name beside it, and it will not be folded into it. Two rods hold
nothing up. They fall outward and the sentence falls between them.

What holds is the **tie** — the little words between the names that pull. A tie
must pull against both ends and settle neither. Where a sentence holds two
things that will not agree, and declines to make them agree, that sentence
stands, and works. Where the tie only sets two names side by side, the sentence
lies down and no one is the worse for it except the caster.

This is why the plain words at the back of the book are worth more than their
price, and why *and* is worth nearly nothing.

Three things break a working and only three. **The rods touch** — one name is
leaned on another until they are the same act, and then there is one act where
two were wanted. **The tie is slack** — a name is set in the sentence that
nothing pulls on, and the whole net goes loose at that one point. **The tension
is settled** — a caster resolves the disagreement he was supposed to be holding,
and it is no longer an incantation but a wish. The third is the ruin of more
good magic than the first two together.

## Of names given by their contrary

Some names are given by their opposite: *near far*, *up down*, *love hate*. They
are in this book and they belong in it, because a world needs them and a person
says them all day.

They will not carry. A thing that is named by what it is not has already settled
its own argument, and there is nothing left in it to pull against. You may put
them in a sentence; you may not build one out of them. A rod made of a resolved
quarrel is a rod with no push in it.

## Of the ties

The ties are marked here in three kinds.

**The plain ties** pull and do not settle: *because*, *if*, *though*, *when*,
*lest*, *whereas*, *provided that*. These are the working ones. Note especially
**because**, which pulls in one direction only and is the finest tie in the
language; and **if**, which pulls on a thing that has not happened yet.

**The side-by-side words** — *and*, *or*, *nor*, and their kind — are not ties
at all in the sense that matters. They set things next to each other. Nothing is
under tension. A sentence made only of them will lie flat however many names you
load onto it.

**The rest** — the small words that fix a name to a place, a time, a person —
are neither, and are used as the sentence requires. The difference between a
working and a charm is very often only these.

## The names

Below, in the order promised: the ground, the doers, the bonds, time, worth,
notions, the inward eye, the passions. Names are set four to a line and marked
apart by a middle dot. A name of several words is one name.

Read it twice. The second time, count.
