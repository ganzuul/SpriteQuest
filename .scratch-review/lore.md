## The forge

**The scaffold kingdom runs a forge that burns room and answers prayers
badly.**

What goes in is a wish — a name, a description, a desire for a crate. What
comes out is *an* artifact: warm, solid, unmistakably the thing you asked for
in the respects you thought to specify, and something else entirely in the
respects you did not. Ask for a crate and you may be handed a crate with a
sword stood upright in it, or a man holding a list of numbers, or a plain
crate and no complaint at all. The forge is not lying. It heard "crate" the
way everyone hears everything: as a suggestion.

**Nothing the forge makes is what was wished for.** The gap is not a defect
of the forge, and the smiths do not apologise for it — it is the forge's
entire contribution. This is the same underdetermination the world already
runs on, made literal in a manufacturing process: *scenes arrive
underdetermined and character resolves them*, and now *artifacts arrive
underdetermined and choice resolves them*.

**The forge burns hot, and the burn is not incidental.** The work cannot be
done in a warm season without cost, so the smiths work when the room is cold.
Heat is the price of artifacts and the schedule follows the weather. In
winter the yard fills. In summer it waits.

**The artifacts come out at low realized detail.** They are serviceable and
legible — closer to a sketch than a finished work, good enough to be used and
not good enough to be admired. Nobody in the scaffold kingdom pretends
otherwise. Scrappiness is not a stage the forge is passing through; it is how
the forge works, and the kingdom has built half its logistics on the
assumption that it always will.

### Provenance

**An artifact becomes real by being chosen.**

A single wish may produce a hundred artifacts, all slightly different, all
*an* answer and none *the* answer. They sit in the yard numbered, and nobody
can say which one is the crate — and until someone can, none of them is.

What makes one of them *the* crate is a decision: someone picks it, assigns it
a role, and writes it into the hollis ledger. **The hollis ledger is the
difference between an artifact and an asset.** Before the entry the object is
one of a hundred
warm approximations. After it, it has a name, a place, and a job — and it is
the entry, not the object, that makes it real.

So provenance here is **earned, not recorded**. The artifact does not become
more detailed when it is chosen. It becomes *more real*, because the world at
last has somewhere to put it.

Two consequences the scaffold kingdom has learned the hard way:

- **Choosing is the expensive step, not making.** Forging is cheap and
  repeatable; a hundred near-identical crates cost less than one decision
  about which crate is the crate. The yard is full because deciding is hard.
- **Nothing is real until it is written down.** An artifact with no hollis ledger
  entry is warm and present and *has no standing* — it cannot be relied on,
  cited, or wrong. This is the comic register the rest of the world already
  runs on: the paperwork is always more finished than the world, and here the
  paperwork is also more *real* than the world.

**Hollis keeps the hollis ledger.** Or keeps, more precisely, the column of it
that balances — the other column is where the forge's output is quietly
promoted,
and it does not balance, and nobody checks it.

### Where this becomes playable

Not settled here — that is `docs/charter.md`'s business (a want, a cost, a
turn), and the raw material is `docs/dev-pain-log.md`. What the forge
guarantees is that the obstruction is *visible*: an underdetermined artifact
in the yard, a hollis ledger that could be entered, and a role waiting to be
assigned.

The player's version of the same feeling is cheap to describe: **you get what
you asked for, and then you have to decide what you got.**
